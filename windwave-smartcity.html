Wind is free, clean and local. The city only has to learn how to catch it.

Overview

Cities use a huge share of the world’s electricity. WindWave looks at how wind turbines, sensors and smart grids can work together to supply some of that power cleanly.

Wind energy turns moving air into electricity. In a smart city, that electricity is not simply produced and used. It is measured, forecast and shared in real time, so supply follows demand.

How it works

Moving air turns the blades of a turbine. The blades spin a shaft connected to a generator, and the generator produces electricity. Urban sites often use smaller turbines on rooftops, towers and open corridors, while larger wind farms outside the city feed the same grid.

Key parts

  • Blades capture the energy in moving air.
  • Generator converts rotation into electricity.
  • Sensors track wind speed, output and turbine health.
  • Storage holds extra power for calm hours.

In a smart city

Smart-city software connects turbines to the wider power network. Weather forecasts predict how much wind power will arrive, and the grid adjusts street lighting, charging stations and building loads to match.

Good planning lets a city use wind power when it blows and save it for when it does not.

Typical uses include street lights, electric-vehicle charging points, metro and bus depots, water pumping and public buildings.

Benefits

  • Produces electricity without burning fuel.
  • Reduces dependence on distant power plants.
  • Lowers running costs once turbines are installed.
  • Pairs well with solar, since wind often blows when the sun does not.

Challenges

Cities have buildings that disturb airflow, so wind is gustier than in open country. Noise, safety, space and cost also need careful planning. Smart monitoring, good siting studies and local storage help reduce these problems.
